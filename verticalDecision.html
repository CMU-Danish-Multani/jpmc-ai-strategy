What each one actually is

Both are about stopping criminals, and people mix them up. They are different jobs, done by different people, paid for out of different budgets. Here is each one as a person at a desk.

Candidate A

Financial crime operations

The rule. Banks are legally required to know who their customers are and to check every payment against government lists of banned people and countries. This is not optional and it is not a product. It is a licence condition.

Job one, the KYC file. KYC means know your customer. Before the bank can serve a company, someone has to build a file proving who that company is, who really owns it behind the shell companies, and how risky it is. Four layers of people touch it: someone gathers the documents, someone drafts the file, someone checks the draft, someone approves it. The file then has to be rebuilt periodically, forever.

Job two, the alert queue. Software checks every payment against sanctions lists. When a name looks like a match it raises an alert, which just means "a human should look at this." Most alerts are wrong. Someone sits with that queue and decides, one at a time, whether each is a real match or a coincidence.

What AI would do. Draft the KYC file from public registries so the human edits rather than writes. And on alerts, write the investigator's summary so the human decides faster.

Candidate D

Consumer fraud and scams

Two different crimes. In fraud, a criminal takes over your account or uses your card without you. In a scam, nobody breaks in: you are tricked into sending the money yourself. That difference decides who pays, which is why it matters strategically.

Job one, the fraud strategy analyst. Every card swipe and transfer gets scored in real time and the system decides in milliseconds: let it through, ask for extra proof, or decline it. This person sets and tunes the rules and models behind that decision.

Job two, the claim handler. When a customer says "that payment was not me", someone investigates and rules on it. The loss then lands either on the bank or is charged back to the merchant.

What AI would do. At the scoring step, replace a fixed threshold with a model. The gain is precision: fewer good customers wrongly declined for the same number of criminals caught. A wrongly declined customer is a real cost too, in complaints and abandoned spending.

The one sentence that separates them. In fraud, the world eventually tells you whether you were right: a chargeback arrives within days and says what actually happened. In financial crime, nobody ever tells you. All you learn is what your own analyst decided, which means the system can only get better at agreeing with itself.

What each choice commits us to

We do not just write Canvas 1. Five more follow, one a week, and the final project re-grades all six together. So the question is not which wins this week, it is which carries a semester.

Why A's lead is smaller than it looks. Five of the six deep research tasks we commissioned were about A. Nothing was commissioned for D. So part of A's score reflects where we happened to look, not where the value is. A's two biggest wins are on the two canvases our research was built to serve.
If we pick A
  • C2 Defensibility: mostly a negative finding. Our own document already withdrew the claim that A builds a proprietary advantage.
  • C3 Sourcing: strong. Vendors resell the same capability to smaller banks through core processors.
  • C4 Learning: argue a codified standard of what a defensible decision looks like, not a data advantage.
  • C5 Authority: the richest material we have. May a machine close an alert with no human record?
  • C6 Synthesis: hard. A Board reads a $38m prize as a rounding error.
If we pick D
  • C2 Defensibility: our strongest canvas. A real feedback loop, plus a measured saturation finding.
  • C3 Sourcing: a genuine three way choice. Build, buy, or co-own with six other banks.
  • C4 Learning: the label is free and fast, so operating experience genuinely compounds.
  • C5 Authority: thinner than A. The EU AI Act exempts fraud detection from its high risk tier.
  • C6 Synthesis: easier. An audited line and a decision about who owns the data.

Making the call

Recommendation: D, and it is close. If someone on the team argues hard for A, they are not being unreasonable. A has better material for one canvas and more research already done. The case for D rests on three things: the money is audited, the feedback is real, and the sourcing question is genuinely interesting.

The honest case for A

Written as if we believed it, because someone should argue this in the room. A has the only genuinely contested authority question in the whole set: whether a machine may close a sanctions alert without leaving a human record, who signs the legal filing that follows, and what happens to the legal protection that filing carries. That is a decision a Board can actually take this year. D has nothing comparable. A also has five of our six research tasks already done, so we would start Canvas 1 with more evidence in hand.
